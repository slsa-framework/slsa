The SLSA project is deprecating
[slsa-github-generator](https://github.com/slsa-framework/slsa-github-generator)
and [slsa-verifier](https://github.com/slsa-framework/slsa-verifier). Both
repositories have carried a "no longer actively maintained" notice since
August 2026. This post explains what the tools did for the project, why the
tooling is evolving, what the next generation looks like, and what the
project will and will not build from here on.

First, the part that matters most. These two tools are the reason SLSA
exists as something you can run rather than something you can read. In
2022 there was no way to get signed, Build Level 3 provenance out of a CI
system without writing it yourself. The generator and the verifier made it
a job you could add to a workflow, and thousands of projects did. They
proved that keyless signing, isolated build jobs and a verifier with real
opinions could work together at scale, and most of what the project knows
about provenance in practice was learned by building and running them. We
owe the people who designed, maintained and reviewed them for years a
great deal of thanks. What follows is the next chapter of it.

The short version: provenance generated by the old tools stays valid and
the reusable workflows keep running until GitHub retires something under
them. But we encourage you to migrate. All development work now goes into
a new stack of smaller pieces: specifications and protobuf definitions at
the bottom, Go libraries on top of them, a command line attester and verifier
built from those libraries, GitHub actions built from those binaries, and
if there is community interest, a thin layer for ecosystem builders at
the very top. We've published a [migration guide] covering the mechanics of
moving.

## What the old tools were

slsa-github-generator started in 2022 as the reference way to get SLSA
Build Level 3 provenance on GitHub Actions. Over time, it grew into three
products in one repository: It generated provenance. It ran your build for
you in reusable workflows that compiled Go, ran Gradle or Maven, packed npm
packages or built container images. And it published, uploading binaries
and provenance to releases and attaching attestations to images in
registries. slsa-verifier was its counterpart: a command line tool, and an
installer action, that checked the provenance against a list of builders it
trusted by construction.

In 2022 that was the right solution. Nothing else existed and the only way
to show that Build Level 3 was reachable on a hosted CI system was to
build every piece of the system and hand users the whole thing. The same
decisions that made the launch possible are the ones that became hard to
carry once the tools had done their job and the world around them moved.

## Why the tooling is evolving

### The tools did more than SLSA asks

SLSA is a specification about what a producer must attest and what a
consumer may conclude. It says nothing about how you actually compile.
The generator did, and had to: to reach its promised level you had to run
your build inside its workflow, with its checkout, its toolchain setup
and its publishing steps, because in 2022 there was no other way to
isolate the signing step from the build. That is no longer true.
The analysis we did before deciding found that of the eight builders, the
container and generic ones only generate provenance, and the Gradle, Maven
and Node.js ones are thin shell wrappers around `./gradlew build`, `mvn
package` and `npm pack`. Users who already had a build pipeline had to
replace it to adopt the tool, and users with anything unusual could not
adopt it at all.

That prescriptiveness also meant the project owned every ecosystem's build
problems. Perhaps not surprisingly, most of those builders ended up with
no active maintainer.

### It was very hard to maintain

The repository holds ten reusable workflows and 36 actions, eight of which
are JavaScript actions that ship a committed `dist/index.js` bundle. Those
eight bundles add up to over 370,000 lines of generated code in the tree,
kept in sync by a workflow of their own.

The pinning rules were the most visible cost to users. The workflows had to
be referenced by tag (as a full `@vX.Y.Z`) and a commit SHA would fail.
That contradicts GitHub's current hardening advice to pin by digest, The
project even had to ship a Renovate configuration telling the bot not to pin its
own actions. On the code side, a shell script walks up to fifty releases
to map a SHA back to a tag and exits with one of seven distinct error codes
when the mapping fails.

Releasing carried the heaviest load. The generator's release checklist has
68 numbered steps. Every generator release ends with a pull request
to the verifier to add that release's provenance to its test data. Every
verifier release ends with a pull request to the generator to update the
verifier hash it pins. Shipping a patch meant walking both repositories and
a third one for end to end tests.

Underneath, both tools were built around the SLSA provenance v0.2 types. The
generator's central build type interface returns v0.2 types directly, and
the literal `https://slsa.dev/provenance/v0.2` is hardcoded everywhere. SLSA v1
support was added later in two places that do not share code with the first,
so there were three separate predicate constructors. The verifier mirrored
that: one Go package per predicate version, a duplicate stack of those packages
for Google Cloud Build, a separate path for npm, eleven builder identifiers
as Go constants, and three Go maps that decided whom to trust. Supporting a
new attestation format or a new builder meant a code change in both projects
and a release of each, with the loop above in between.

### Maintainer involvement changed

The git history tells the rest. Counting commits on the main branch, with
bot authors separated out:

| Year | Generator (human) | Generator (bot) | Verifier (human) | Verifier (bot)
| --- | --- | --- | --- | ---
| 2022 | 344 | 97 | 145 | 85
| 2023 | 403 | 99 | 97 | 60
| 2024 | 66 | 34 | 36 | 40
| 2025 | 19 | 14 | 9 | 17

The last feature in the generator landed in February 2025
(Sigstore bundle support), followed by the last release (v2.1.0). The last
code change in the verifier landed in June 2025, with release v2.7.1 the
same week. By 2025 half of the remaining human commits were dependency
bumps, and the dependency bots themselves stopped being merged in mid 2025.

The reviewer pool shrank faster than the commit count. In 2022, fifteen
different people approved pull requests on the generator and ten on the
verifier. In 2026 it is two on each. Today the generator has 36 open pull
requests, twenty of them from bots, and 261 open issues, 105 of which have
never received a comment. The verifier has 24 open pull requests and 118
open issues, 46 without a reply. The oldest open pull requests date from
2023.

This is the normal life of open source. The people who built these tools
carried a very large surface area for years while companies and individuals
able to fund that work changed. A surface that size needs a team that size.
When the team is smaller, the right response is not to ask fewer people to
carry the same weight, but to change the weight that is carried. That is
what the rest of this post is about.

## The Tooling Cake

At its core, the SLSA evolution means we move from maintaining products to
maintaining the foundations those products were built from. The plan is a
stack of five layers. Each built only from the layer below it. Each usable
on its own:

#### Docs and definitions

The specification, the protobuf definitions of every attestation format, and
agent skills that describe the attesting and verification processes. The
protobuf definitions now live in
[slsa-framework/protos](https://github.com/slsa-framework/protos) and are
the single source of truth for the types every layer above uses.

#### Developer tools

Go type libraries generated from those definitions, plus libraries to generate,
sign and verify attestations, and conformance validators so a third party
implementation in any language can check that it produces and consumes the
formats correctly. The
[attester](https://github.com/slsa-framework/attester) and
[verifier](https://github.com/slsa-framework/verifier) repositories are
libraries first.

#### Command line utilities

The attester, the verifier and sourcetool are thin binaries over those libraries.
The attester generates build provenance and VSAs, signs with Sigstore or keys,
and in GitHub Actions can watch a workflow run and attest the artifacts it
produced. Artifacts can be read as subjects from binaries, workflow artifacts,
release assets and SBOMs. The verifier checks build provenance, source provenance
and VSAs with one command each, and computes the SLSA level from a catalog of
controls written in YAML and CEL rather than hardcoded in Go: the catalog is 31
files, and the builder registry that binds a builder identity to its signer is
an 83 line YAML file listing seven builders, the old generator's workflows among
them. Adding a builder or a check is a data change.

#### Composable services

The actions in
[slsa-framework/actions](https://github.com/slsa-framework/actions): a
reusable workflow that attests a run, actions that compute and attest SLSA
source levels, and actions that verify build provenance, source provenance
and VSAs in CI. That repository contains no Go and no TypeScript. It is
four workflows and eight composite actions, and each action verifies the
binary it downloads against the provenance published with its release
before running it.

#### Integration framework

The top of the later stack is where ecosystem builders belong and, from now on,
the project will not own them. An ecosystem-specific builder that wants SLSA
provenance calls the attester, and the layer's job is to make that easy. Any
builders are to be maintained by the communities that need them.

The arithmetic of the change is simple. The attester is about 3,700 lines
of Go outside tests. The actions repository has no compiled code at all. We are
reducing the reponsibility and hence, the lines of code, maintained by the SLSA
project to focus on the foundations to help ecosystems succeed.

## Initial adoption signs

The stack is already running in production. The Kubernetes image promoter,
which copies every Kubernetes image to production and signs it, was
[rewritten this year](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2026/03/17/image-promoter-rewrite/)
with provenance verification on by default. It verifies the provenance of
staging images with the new verifier's library, and since
[this week](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/promo-tools/pull/2050) it
builds its Verification Summary Attestations with that same library
instead of assembling the statements by hand. The attester is at work in
other parts of the Kubernetes release pipeline, including generating its own
provenance. In each case the project imported a layer of the cake rather than
adopting a workflow, which is exactly what the layers are for.

## What this means for you

If you produce provenance with `slsa-github-generator`, the generic
generator's job becomes the attester's reusable workflow with no hashes to
compute: artifacts you uploaded are attested automatically. If you used the
Go builder, you build with whatever you like and let the attester observe
the run. If you consume provenance with `slsa-verifier`, the new verifier
reads everything the old generator ever produced, so you can switch
verifiers before any producer has moved, and you name the builders you
trust instead of inheriting a list. The [migration guide] has the before and
after for every workflow and every flag.

GitHub's own artifact attestations remain a good choice for projects that
live entirely on GitHub and verify with the GitHub CLI. SLSA's mission is
the whole software supply chain, on every platform, and the layers below
the actions are what make that possible. The actions are the frosting. The
cake is our new set of libraries.

To everyone who built, reviewed, released and debugged `slsa-github-generator`
and `slsa-verifier`: thank you. The new stack stands on what you shipped,
and will keep verifying your provenance in the years to come.
